# The Japanese language decks — the plan (Oct 2026)

**Status: BUILT (2026-10-30).** All nine decks are in `decks/`: the five JLPT levels, hiragana, katakana,
phrases and idioms. They were generated by `.claude/jlpt/`, whose working notes and findings now live in that
pipeline's entry in `docs/lang-decks.md`. **Read that entry before touching the generator.** This file keeps the
plan as it was written, the owner's answers (§9) and what building it changed (§10).

---

## 1. "The same form": what every language collection on the shelf already does

Measured from the shipped decks (`decks/Mandarin-HSK-3.0-Level-1.folio-deck.json`, the UKBI and CAPLE sets)
and from `.claude/build-lang-decks.js`. The Japanese collection must match each point:

- **One file per exam level**, `decks/<EXAM>-<level>-<Language>.folio-deck.json`, each fetched on its own.
  The Mandarin set was one file and was split, on request, for exactly this reason (`lang-decks.md`, "THE NINE
  WERE ONE DECK"). The language's English name has to appear in the file name, because `build-lang-decks.js`
  matches a file to its language by that name. An unmatched file is an error.
- **One extra deck of phrases and expressions** beside the levels. Every language has one: Mandarin Everyday
  phrases, Spanish Phrases, Indonesian Phrases and Expressions, and so on. Mandarin also has Idioms.
- **Every word is ONE note with TWO cards**: target → English and English → target. Each direction is scheduled
  on its own (`lang-decks.md`, "EVERY WORD IS ONE NOTE WITH TWO CARDS").
- **One note type per language**, declared in `meta.types`, with its `speechLang` set (`zh-CN` for `hsk`, so
  `ja-JP` here). The answer side carries a speaker (`uc-tts`, `data-say`), the word broken into its parts, the
  compounds built on it, and a fold of real example sentences ("In a sentence") with the word picked out.
- **A level is taught on top of the levels below it.** Those lower levels are read out of the SHIPPED deck
  files, so a word is carded once, at the lowest level that lists it. One level is built per run, and
  re-running the generator reproduces every shipped level byte for byte.
- **The deck's `desc` states its sources and its method in plain words**, including what it could not get.
  The UKBI description says outright that its exam board publishes no list.
- **Ids**: the deck id is short and lower-case (`hsk30l1`, `ukbi1`), and card ids are `u_<deckid>_<n>`.
- **A combined all-levels file, if one is made, is gitignored** and regenerated by a `combine.py`.
- **A deck is community content**: it gets no changelog line and no version bump. The APP changes it forces
  do get one (§6).

## 2. The fact the whole pipeline rests on: THE JLPT PUBLISHES NO WORD LIST

This was **established, not assumed**. The Japanese-Language Proficiency Test (run by the Japan Foundation and
JEES) has five levels, N5 (lowest) to N1 (highest), since its 2010 revision. Its own FAQ
(https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/index.html, read 2026-10-30) answers "Why is 'Test Content Specifications' no longer
available after the 2010 revision of the JLPT?". The board's answer is that publishing a list of vocabulary,
kanji and grammar items "was not necessarily appropriate", and it points to its level summaries and sample
questions instead.

So Japanese is in the **UKBI / CAPLE position, not the HSK one**: there is no official list to read. Every
"JLPT N5 word list" a learner finds is a third-party compilation, usually built from the pre-2010 four-level
specifications and mapped onto the five new levels. **The deck's description must say this, in those words.**

What the board does publish is the **level summary** (https://www.jlpt.jp/e/about/levelsummary.html). It gives
one line per level, and these lines are the SCOPE of each deck, used the way `ukbi_level.py` uses UKBI's
descriptors. Summarised here, not reproduced:

| level | the board's summary (paraphrased) | what it implies for the inventory |
|---|---|---|
| N5 | some basic Japanese: typical expressions in hiragana, katakana and basic kanji; slow classroom and daily-life talk | survival and the classroom: greetings, numbers, time, family, food, places, the closed classes |
| N4 | basic Japanese: passages on familiar daily topics in basic vocabulary and kanji; slow everyday conversation | daily life widened: the home, errands, travel, health, plans and feelings |
| N3 | everyday Japanese to a certain degree: everyday written material, newspaper **headlines**, near-natural speech | the bridge level: headline vocabulary, the workplace's surface, abstract connectives |
| N2 | everyday Japanese plus a variety of circumstances: newspaper and magazine articles, simple critiques, news reports | the press and the office: society, economy, institutions, argument |
| N1 | Japanese in a variety of circumstances: editorials, critiques, logically complex and abstract writing, lectures | the editorial and academic register: abstract Sino-Japanese vocabulary, formal written forms |

The UKBI lesson applies here: **read each level's summary against the one below it** before writing that
level's inventory. N3 is the first level to name newspapers (headlines only). N2 names articles, and N1 names
editorials. That chain is the deck boundary.

## 3. Where the words come from — options and a recommendation

**DECIDE (Q1).** There are three ways to choose which words go in each level:

- **(a) A community JLPT list, credited.** The widely used set is Jonathan Waller's lists (tanos.co.uk). They
  are commonly described as Creative Commons Attribution, but **this was not verified**: the site did not
  answer from this container on 2026-10-30. The first step of Phase 0 is to read the licence on the site
  itself. If it is open, this is the closest thing to what learners mean by "the N5 words", and the deck says
  plainly that the list is unofficial.
- **(b) Assembled, the UKBI way.** Each level's scope comes from the board's summary (§2). The words within
  that scope are ordered by frequency and checked against the dictionary's own commonness markers (JMdict's
  `news1` / `ichi1` / `spec1` / `gai1` priority tags). This is honest and reproducible, but it will not match
  any list a learner compares it with.
- **(c) (a), checked by (b).** The community list sets the membership. Frequency and the JMdict markers only
  ORDER the words and FLAG outliers for a human to read: a word the list puts at N5 that the corpus barely
  uses, or a very common word missing from every level.

**Recommendation: (c)** if the licence in (a) is confirmed, otherwise **(b)**. Whichever is chosen, it goes
into the deck description.

### Measured already: the obvious frequency list is broken for Japanese

The other pipelines rank words with hermitdave/FrequencyWords (OpenSubtitles 2018, CC BY-SA 4.0). For
Japanese that repo has **only `ja_full.txt`, with no `ja_50k.txt`**, and the file is segmented in a way that
cuts verbs and adjectives off at their kana endings (okurigana). Its top rows include **`言`, `多` and `始`**:
the stems of 言う, 多い and 始める, not words. So **it cannot be used as it stands**. A Japanese frequency
ranking has to be MADE by segmenting a corpus with a morphological analyser. For example, fugashi with UniDic,
run over the Japanese half of Tatoeba and/or the OpenSubtitles text, counting lemmas rather than surface forms.
That is allowed: CLAUDE.md lets an offline generator use any library, installed outside the repo, because only
its output ships. **Check the analyser's and the dictionary's licences before using either one** (UniDic has
been distributed under a choice of licences; read the current terms rather than trusting this line).

## 4. The sources, with what is known about each licence

| what | source | licence | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| headwords, readings, English senses, part of speech, "usually kana" (`uk`), commonness markers | JMdict (EDRDG) | CC BY-SA 4.0 | **confirmed** on https://www.edrdg.org/edrdg/licence.html, 2026-10-30 |
| per-kanji meanings, on/kun readings, stroke count, school grade, frequency | KANJIDIC2 (EDRDG) | CC BY-SA 4.0, **with special conditions** (§8 of that page): the SKIP codes are Jack Halpern's, under his own terms | **confirmed**. **Do not ship SKIP codes**, which the cards do not need anyway |
| example sentences | Tatoeba | CC BY 2.0 FR | already used by every pipeline here. The attribution goes into the deck description, as in HSK's |
| a kanji's parts (the equivalent of HSK's Make Me a Hanzi breakdown) | KanjiVG | believed CC BY-SA 3.0 | **unverified**: GitHub refused the request from here. Read the repo's licence file before use |
| furigana alignment (which kana sits over which kanji) | JmdictFurigana | believed CC BY-SA | **unverified**. Alternatively, derive it from JMdict's readings plus KANJIDIC2's readings |
| senses and inflection tables, as a cross-check | Wiktionary via kaikki.org | CC BY-SA / GFDL | already used by CAPLE and UKBI. kaikki.org answered for Japanese |
| level membership | §3, Q1 | — | **open** |
| frequency | made here (§3) | the corpus's own licence | **to build** |
| pitch accent | none confirmed open | — | **left out** unless an openly licensed source is found and confirmed. No accent is guessed |

A source that cannot be confirmed open is left out, and the deck says what is missing. **No reading, accent,
gloss or example is invented.** If there is no example sentence, the card shows no fold. If there is no kanji
breakdown, the card shows no breakdown.

Each note is two cards. The table above was revised to match what was built: the plan had one kana deck and a
yojijukugo-only idiom deck.

The subtitle follows HSK's model: "The first N words — start here if you are new to Japanese", then
"N more words, taking you to about M — study after N5". **The counts are read off the built deck, never typed
in advance.**

### The levels run DOWNWARD, as UKBI's do

The JLPT counts N5 → N1 as a learner climbs, which is the same inversion UKBI has (`lang-decks.md`, "THE LEVELS
ARE NUMBERED FROM THE BOTTOM"). `levelRank` in `build-lang-decks.js` knows CEFR, UKBI and HSK and nothing else,
so a JLPT title would rank 90 and sort ALPHABETICALLY: N1, N2, N3, N4, N5. That is upside down, and nothing
would report it. **The pipeline's first catalogue change is a rule ranking `/\bJLPT N([1-5])\b/` as `5 − n`,
placed before the generic `Levels? (\d)` test**, plus a `LANGS` row `{ word: "Japanese", name: "Japanese",
l1: … }`. The l1 figure is a published first-language count, filled in from a source at build time and not
from memory. It places Japanese between Portuguese and German.

### The note type `jlpt` (proposed fields)

`Expression` (the written form) · `Reading` (kana) · `Furigana` (ruby markup, §6) · `Romaji` (Q2) ·
`English` (senses grouped as HSK groups them, part of speech in `uc-pos`) · `Kanji` (one row per kanji:
meaning, on and kun readings, and its parts with their meanings, the counterpart of HSK's `Characters`) ·
`Examples` (Tatoeba, the word in bold, the reading available, the English beneath) · `Say` · `Compounds`
(words built on the same kanji, from the deck's own levels first).

- **Front of the Japanese → English card**: the form a Japanese reader would actually write. JMdict's `uk` tag
  ("usually written using kana alone") decides it. よろしく and たくさん are shown in kana, with the kanji
  spelling on the back. 学校 is shown in kanji. This mirrors how HSK hides an identical traditional form.
- **Front of the English → Japanese card**: "Say it in Japanese" over the English, as HSK's reverse card does.
- **Counters and verb class.** A verb's class (godan / ichidan / irregular) and transitivity, and a noun's usual
  counter, play the role of HSK's `Measure word`: shown when the dictionary gives them, absent otherwise.
- **Register.** Examples are tagged polite (です/ます) or plain where the sentence makes it plain. A word that
  belongs to one register (an honorific, a humble form, slang) says so in the gloss, taken from JMdict's own
  `hon` / `hum` / `pol` / `col` / `sl` tags. UKBI's warning applies: `informal` and `colloquial` are not the
  same tag (`lang-decks.md`).

### What carries across from the Mandarin pipeline unchanged

The two-card note, the "In a sentence" fold, the bold word in the example, the speaker, the compounds list, the
rule that a gloss with no source is not shown, and the HSK finding that **a subdeck name is a string**: count
the distinct subdecks after every build and read the list.

## 6. Changes to the APP this collection forces (each one reader-facing, so each gets a changelog line)

1. **Furigana needs `<ruby>`, `<rt>` and `<rp>` in the deck sanitizer.** `SANITIZE_TAGS` in `app.js` does not
   list them today, so a deck's furigana would be stripped silently on import. Adding them is a sanitizer
   change: **bump `SANITIZE_REV`** (CLAUDE.md, community decks), add a test that a ruby block survives
   `uDeckNormalize`, and read `docs/community-decks.md` first. The fallback, if the owner declines, is the
   reading on its own line, as pinyin is shown today.
2. **`ja-JP` speech.** `SPEECH_LANG_RX` already accepts it (checked). What remains is to check that the word
   is spoken from the kana or the expression, not from romaji, and that the device has a Japanese voice.
3. **Fonts.** No webfont is added (CLAUDE.md: no remote dependencies). Japanese falls back to the system CJK
   font. A screenshot on a phone checks that kanji, kana and ruby render at the card's sizes.
4. **The catalogue's level rule and `LANGS` row** (§5). This is a tooling change, but it decides what the
   Collections page shows.

## 7. The pipeline (proposed): `.claude/jlpt/`

Python, like `.claude/ukbi/` and `.claude/caple/`, for their reason: a further level is a re-run, not a
rebuild. **One level per run**, caching its corpora in a gitignored `.claude/jlpt-cache/`.

1. `fetch` — JMdict, KANJIDIC2, Tatoeba (jpn + eng + links), KanjiVG, and the level list per Q1. Each is pinned
   by date and hash.
2. `extract` — JMdict to headword records (forms, readings, senses, tags, priority), and KANJIDIC2 to kanji
   records (dropping SKIP).
3. `freq` — segment the corpus with the morphological analyser and count lemmas (§3). Written to the cache, and
   re-run only by request.
4. `select` — the level's membership (Q1), minus every word already carded in the SHIPPED lower decks, ordered
   by frequency.
5. `examples` — Tatoeba sentences containing the lemma (matched through the analyser, never by substring, so
   言う is not matched inside 言葉). Each gets the HSK fit checks, at most three per word, and the word is put in
   bold.
6. `kanji` — the per-kanji rows and the compounds.
7. `emit` — the `folio-deck` JSON, ids `u_jlptn<k>_<n>`, the description assembled from the stated sources and
   counts.

The browser half is `check-jlpt.js [n5…n1|phr]`, after `check-ukbi.js`. It imports the deck through the
Studio, opens both directions of a sample of cards, and asserts that furigana survives import, that a `uk`
word fronts in kana, that no card is missing its English, that the example fold holds the bold word, and that
the reading matches JMdict. Then run `node .claude/build-lang-decks.js`, `test-lang-decks.js` and
`test-subdecks.js`.

## 10. What building it changed from the plan

- **The list's dictionary joins are checked, not trusted** (`resolve.py`). Ten joins pointed at the wrong
  homograph: コップ was joined to "cop", これ to "hey", ボタン to the tree peony. Moving every row whose
  definition and entry disagree would be wrong in the other direction, because some of Waller's own
  definitions are the mistake (そっと "face of the earth", どうか "copper coin"). So a row moves only to an
  entry marked common for that spelling, and only when the joined entry is not.
- **The frequency order is made, not downloaded**, as §3 said it must be. The Tatoeba corpus is segmented
  with MeCab and UniDic (BSD; installed outside the repo; nothing of it ships).
- **The analyser's readings are not trusted over the dictionary's.** It reads the 母 in お母さん as はは and
  the 日 in 月曜日 as ひ. So the headword inside an example sentence carries the dictionary's furigana, and a
  kanji spelling counts as a match without a reading check, except for the 165 spellings two common words
  share (一日 いちにち / ついたち). It also reads 私 as the formal わたくし everywhere, so example sentences
  show わたし.
- **Romaji spells the particles by sound** (は "wa", へ "e", を "o"), but only where the segmentation agrees
  with the dictionary's reading. Long vowels follow the kana (おう is "ou"), because kana alone cannot say
  whether おう is one long vowel or two.
- **Pitch accent is left out**: no openly licensed source was confirmed.
